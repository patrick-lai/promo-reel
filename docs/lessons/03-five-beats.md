# Lesson 3: Hook, problem, demo, payoff, call to action

**The principle.** Most promos that work, from a 30 s clip to a 2 minute explainer, move through five beats, and each beat has one job. The **hook** stops the scroll (lesson 1). The **problem** makes the viewer recognise a pain they already have, so the demo lands as an answer instead of a feature tour. The **demo** is the proof, real footage of the thing doing the job. The **payoff** shows life afterwards, the feeling you're actually selling. The **call to action** gives that feeling somewhere to go: one verb, one place.

Why it works: without a problem, a demo is just a product doing things, and the viewer has to work out why they'd care. Without a payoff, they've seen the steps but not the result. And without a call to action, any interest you built leaks away the moment the video ends. For a 60 s hero, our style guide already gives rough slots: hook 0 to 4 s, problem and first UI 4 to 20 s, features 20 to 42 s, a montage peak 42 to 52 s, and the end card from 52 s.

**Example.** Dropbox's 2009 explainer, made by Common Craft ([a re-upload on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xy9nSnalvPc), 2:17), is the textbook case. Dropbox told IdeaRocket it was viewed about 30,000 times a day on its homepage ([their write-up](https://idearocketanimation.com/6164-can-learn-dropboxs-famous-explainer-video/)). The beats are easy to time from the captions:
- Hook at 0:02: "you've been there, you're about to buy lunch and realise your wallet is in your other pants."
- Problem from 0:04 to 0:35: "the problem is organisation", then "if you have more than one [computer], keeping track of all of your files can be a pain."
- Demo from 0:35 to 1:35: Josh, his trip files spread across a laptop, desktop and phone, "tired of having to email files to himself."
- Payoff from 1:35 to 2:08: the trip is a success, his mum gets the photos, and you can "stop worrying about managing files and backups."
- Call to action at 2:08: "you can download Dropbox now at getdropbox.com."

Devin's launch, from our reference study, shows that you can bend this. It skips the problem and spends most of its 110 s on one continuous demo. But it still ends on a concrete ask at about 1:34: "if you'd like to try out Devin on your own real world tasks, send us a request below."

**Our own cuts.** Mapped onto the five beats, hero v5 has two strong beats and two weak ones.
- The hook is the wordmark plus "Your AI dev crew". It's clear, but it's a label rather than a promise.
- There's no problem beat. We go straight from the wordmark to "Tell it what you want built."
- The demo is solid: plan, crew, worktrees, checks, "It only asks when it needs you", the reviewer.
- The payoff is our best line, "Go to bed. Wake up to merged PRs." But then "your workshop grows" and "Every token builds something" come straight after it and soften it.
- The call to action is a "macOS alpha" pill with no URL and no verb. That's a status, not an action. Talk show v7's end card is the same, and anime v11 implies its problem and payoff through night turning to morning, but it also ends on no ask.

**For the next commission-ai video.** Write the five beats as five single lines before the shot list, and check each shot against its line in the stills round. Two things only you can settle. First, the problem line has to be something true that our viewers feel. One option, not a decision, is the evening spent babysitting agents instead of sleeping, which sets up "Go to bed" perfectly. Second, the call to action needs a real destination. The end-card URL is still open, and until there's a waitlist, download or repo to point at, the end card can only name the product. Let the payoff be the last spoken line, so nothing comes between it and the end card.
